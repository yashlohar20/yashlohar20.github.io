Hardware-in-the-Loop Test Equipment

One board. Three sensor protocols.

A configurable multi-sensor emulation platform that lets an ECU or controller believe it is wired to a real pressure, temperature or flow sensor, with dynamic process profiles instead of static values.

Role Sole authorProgramme M.Eng. thesis, Hochschule SchmalkaldenStatus Design phase, topic confirmedTools STM32H7 · STM32G4 · C
In plain words

A box that pretends to be a sensor. A test engineer plugs it into a car or machine controller and tells it what the “sensor” should read, so the controller can be tested without the real machine.

The problem

A controller (ECU) must be tested against sensor signals that change over time and sometimes fail. Commercial test systems split this across separate, expensive, closed modules, and I found no open-source design that covers the three most common sensor signal types on one board.

  • Firmware logic for time-varying profiles (ramps, steps, sine, recorded playback).
  • Interactive browser demos and a 57-sensor reference sheet covering five industries.
  • Scope agreed with my supervisor, with risky parts deliberately cut.

Why this tool does not exist yet

Testing an ECU or industrial controller needs something that behaves like its sensors. Commercial hardware-in-the-loop systems (dSPACE, NI, Vector, Pickering) split these features across separate, expensive proprietary modules. A literature review of six key papers found no open-source design that puts 0–5 V, 4–20 mA and SENT on one board together with dynamic profiles. That gap is the thesis.

Behaving like a real process

A static value is not enough: a real pump does not hold 4.5 bar forever. The master reads a profile table every 10 ms and interpolates the output, so it can play a pressure drop and recovery, an engine-start ramp, a sudden pump failure, slow sensor drift or a sine oscillation, and later play back recorded real-world data. This is firmware only and needs no extra hardware.

Master, channel boards, protocols

An STM32H7 master (developed on a Nucleo-H743ZI2) owns the profiles and timing and talks to PC software over USB serial. Channel boards built around the STM32G431 generate the voltage, current-loop and SENT signals. Boards are ordered fully assembled from JLCPCB. The same hardware serves other sensor types by changing the conversion formula, which is how pressure, temperature, flow and level are covered.

What I cut, and why

CAN-FD was removed from the build and replaced by USB serial after weighing firmware risk; CAN-FD stays in the thesis as a theoretical description. Fault injection moved to future scope because it needs dedicated ECU hardware and relays on every channel. Assembly was moved to JLCPCB to remove the risk of hand-soldering a fine-pitch MCU. Each cut was made on purpose, and recorded in a decision log.

Status: requirements and architecture are defined, the supervisor has confirmed the topic, and the next step is hardware ordering and schematic capture.

Handheld Console

Power, from a single cell.

Buck and boost conversion, charge management, and protection — every value sized and justified from a datasheet, not copied from a reference design.

Role Sole designerType Self-initiated projectStatus Schematic complete, layout in progressTools KiCad · LTspice
In plain words

The power system of a handheld game console: how to charge a lithium battery safely from USB-C and turn it into the 3.3 V and 5 V rails the electronics need.

The problem

A handheld lives on a small battery, so wasted power is lost play time and heat. Stepping 9 V down to 3.3 V would waste about 63% as heat, while converting directly from the 3.7 V cell loses only about 10%.

What I did
  • Chose a switching buck (3.7 V to 3.3 V) over a linear regulator after thermal and efficiency calculations, and a boost converter for the 5 V rail.
  • Designed USB-C charging (TP4056) and protection (DW01A and FS8205A) against over-current, over-discharge and over-charge.
  • Built custom KiCad symbols and footprints from datasheets and simulated the converters in LTspice.
  • Reviewed my own schematic line by line and logged 11 mistakes with the reason and fix for each.
Result

Schematic complete with zero ERC errors, with full documentation kept throughout.

Power-topology trade-offs, battery charging and protection, schematic review, library creation, documentation.

From the design log — real mistakes, real fixes
Issue
CC1 and CC2 on the USB-C connector shared a single pulldown resistor instead of one each.
Fix
Gave each its own independent 5.1kΩ to GND — without this handshake, a USB-C charger supplies no power at all.
Issue
TP4056's Chip Enable (CE) pin was wired to GND.
Fix
CE is active-HIGH — tied it to VCC instead. Wired LOW, the charger IC never turns on.
Issue
Current-sense pin (CS) on the DW01A connected to the wrong side of the sense resistor R5.
Fix
Moved CS to the high side of R5 — on the wrong side, the IC reads 0V across R5 always, silently disabling overcurrent protection.
Issue
EPAD (thermal pad) on the TP4056 left unconnected.
Fix
Tied to GND — EPAD is both the heat path and an internal ground connection; floating it risks overheating and ERC errors.

Why USB + LiPo, not a 9V input

Stepping 9V down to 3.3V wastes roughly 63% of the energy as heat. Stepping 3.7V (a single LiPo cell) down to 3.3V loses almost nothing — around 90% efficient. Every commercial handheld runs USB charging into a LiPo for exactly this reason, so that's the architecture this console uses too: USB-C charges the battery via a TP4056, and the battery is the only thing that ever powers the rest of the board.

Buck, boost, and why not just a MOSFET

A MOSFET wired as a variable resistor (a linear regulator) can drop 3.7V to 3.3V, but it burns the difference as heat proportional to current — in a sealed handheld with no airflow, that heat has nowhere to go. A buck converter instead switches a transistor on and off about a million times a second; the ratio of on-time to off-time (duty cycle) sets the average output voltage, and an inductor plus capacitor smooth that into clean DC — around 90% efficient with almost no wasted heat.

Protection is a control problem, not just a component

The DW01A doesn't switch the battery current itself — it's the brain. It reads current through a sense resistor and battery voltage directly, then drives the gates of two back-to-back FS8205A MOSFETs that actually carry the current. Two independent gates exist because overdischarge and overcurrent are separate fault conditions that need independent responses — a single shared gate can't do that.

Motor Drive

Three phases. One inverter.

A 48V isolated BLDC driver shield, captured in KiCad: six discrete MOSFETs, isolated dual-channel gate drivers and a current sensor on every phase.

Role Sole designerType Independent projectStatus Schematic complete, layout nextTools KiCad 9 · LTspice
In plain words

The electronics that turn a 48 V battery into the three-phase power a brushless motor needs, like the drive in an e-bike or a robot joint, with the control side electrically isolated for safety.

The problem

Switching 48 V at high current is unforgiving. If the top and bottom switch in one leg are ever on together, the battery is shorted. The controller also has to be protected from the power stage.

What I did

  • Chose isolated gate drivers, 150 V MOSFETs and a current-sense amplifier per phase from their datasheets.
  • Simulated the switching in LTspice, found a shoot-through failure with zero dead-time and fixed it.
  • Sized the gate-driver supply capacitor from the MOSFET gate charge.

From the design log: a real failure, a real fix
Issue
First half-bridge simulation ran with zero dead-time. Both MOSFETs conducted during each transition and the 48V bus was shorted through the leg (shoot-through).
Fix
Added a dead-time gap of about 500 ns between one switch turning off and the other turning on, and chose a gate driver where that gap is set by a single resistor (49.9 kΩ on DT) rather than in firmware.

Why an isolated dual-channel driver

The UCC21520 has two driver channels with 5.7 kVRMS isolation, so the 48V power stage stays galvanically separate from the controller side. The PWM inputs per phase are INA/INB (high and low), a DIS pin per phase can shut the leg down, and the DT pin takes one resistor that programs the dead-time. Each MOSFET has its own 10 Ω gate resistor to trade switching speed against ringing.

The gap that stops a short circuit

In a half-bridge the top and bottom switch must never conduct together. LTspice showed the shoot-through current with zero dead-time and a clean transition with roughly 500 ns. The cost is a small, predictable amount of diode conduction during the gap, which is why the value is a deliberate choice and not simply "as long as possible".

Why 150V devices on a 48V bus

Switching an inductive load causes overshoot when the current is interrupted: the LTspice model showed spikes up to about 57V. The IRFS4115 is rated 150V, which leaves more than 2.5× margin above that spike, so the devices are not stressed on every switching edge. The simulation used a 75V device, so the real board has more headroom than the simulated one.

Sizing the supply capacitor from gate charge

The driver supply capacitor is sized from the gate charge instead of guessed: C ≥ 2 × Qg ÷ ΔV. With the IRFS4115 at Qg = 120 nC (max) and a droop target of 0.5V, that gives 480 nF, so the schematic uses a 1 µF capacitor with a 220 nF capacitor beside it for local high-frequency bypass.

One shunt and amplifier per phase

Each phase has a 1 mΩ shunt read by an INA240A2, a 50 V/V amplifier with a −4 to 80V common-mode range, built for PWM-switched loads. 50A through 1 mΩ is 50 mV, which the amplifier turns into 2.5V for the ADC. The three ISENSE signals plus power-good go to the control interface.

Device Characterization · Simulation

Measuring what the datasheet only summarizes.

A simulated double-pulse test, built in LTspice and cross-checked in MATLAB, extracting turn-on and turn-off switching energy — the same qualification method used to validate SiC and GaN devices for EV traction inverters.

Role Sole authorType Simulation studyStatus Complete (no hardware yet)Tools LTspice · MATLAB
In plain words

Before building a power stage, measure how much energy a switch wastes each time it turns on or off. I did this in software, using a real manufacturer model of the transistor.

The problem

Datasheet switching curves are measured under conditions that are not mine. The double-pulse test is the standard way to measure switching energy under my own bus voltage, current and inductor.

What I did
  • Built the test in LTspice with an Infineon IPP023NE7N3 model at 48 V with a 1 mH inductor: a long first pulse ramps the current to about 4.8 A, then a short second pulse is measured.
  • Fixed a floating gate reference and a SPICE initial-condition trap along the way.
  • Rebuilt the test independently in MATLAB with a simplified physics model to cross-check the result.
Result

Eon = 203 µJ and Eoff = 52.1 µJ at about 4.8 A. The roughly 4× difference is explained by diode reverse recovery. These are simulation results, not bench measurements.

What a double-pulse test actually measures

Instead of continuous PWM, exactly two gate pulses are fired at the MOSFET under test. The first ramps current up to a known level through an inductor, standing in for a motor winding or filter choke. A short off-period lets that current freewheel through the second MOSFET's body diode. The second pulse then switches back on directly into that freewheeling current — the worst-case real-world switching event, where the device must support voltage, ramp current, and sweep out reverse-recovery charge all at once.

Why it matters for the SiC/GaN transition

Every MOSFET datasheet has an Eon/Eoff switching-energy curve — the double-pulse test is the industry-standard method used to actually generate those numbers, rather than just read them. As EV and renewable-energy power electronics shift from silicon to SiC and GaN devices switching an order of magnitude faster, this is the qualification test used to validate real Eon/Eoff loss, size heatsinks correctly, and catch dangerous ringing before it becomes a field failure.

Two independent implementations, cross-checked

Modeled in LTspice using a real Infineon SPICE device model — debugging a floating gate-reference and a SPICE steady-state initial-condition trap along the way — and separately in MATLAB with a simplified first-principles physics model, to confirm the two approaches agreed.

Why turn-on costs about four times more than turn-off

At roughly 4.8 A the extracted energies were Eon = 203 µJ and Eoff = 52.1 µJ. The asymmetry comes from the body diode of the opposite MOSFET: when the switch turns on, the diode has to recover its stored charge and that reverse-recovery current flows through the switch while the full bus voltage is across it. This is simulation data from an Infineon IPP023NE7N3 model, not a bench measurement.

Finding zero before drawing anything

The plotter doesn't know where it is on power-up, so homing runs first: drive X to its right limit switch, then to its left limit switch (measuring full travel in the process), repeat for Y top/bottom, move to the calculated bottom-left origin, then back off 1 second to clear the switch cleanly. Six deliberate steps, not a single "go to zero" — because measuring full travel on the way is what makes every later move a known fraction of that distance rather than a guess.

A motor driver pin that doubles as a boot-mode pin

GPIO12 on the ESP32 is one of its documented strapping pins — sampled once at reset to choose the flash's operating voltage. This board uses GPIO12 to drive one motor-driver input (XIN2). If that pin is pulled high at the moment of reset, the chip can latch into the wrong flash voltage mode and fail to boot at all — a fault that looks electrical but is actually a timing conflict between the motor circuit and the ESP32's own boot sequence. Diagnosing it meant reading the ESP32 datasheet's strapping-pin table line by line rather than assuming the fault was a wiring mistake, and resolving it validated the fix over repeated power-cycle tests.

Tuning beats redesigning

The biggest precision gain didn't come from new hardware — it came from discovering the X-axis needed asymmetric PWM values (220 right, 200 left) to compensate for friction asymmetry in the rail, found only by logging deviation data across 25 runs rather than trusting the first result. Emergency-stop checks are embedded inside every motion loop, not just at the top level, because a physical system has to be able to stop mid-move, not just before starting one.

One thin board instead of a stack

A XIAO module on a carrier board with a USB-C connector is about 5 mm of electronics. One custom board is about 2–2.5 mm, which matters on a wrist. The schematic follows Espressif’s hardware design guidelines and the XIAO ESP32-S3 reference design, so the same firmware runs on the custom board.

Four layers, a solid ground, a 50 Ω trace

Espressif recommends a solid ground plane under the radio and crystal, which is why the board has four layers. The antenna feed is a controlled-impedance trace: 0.357 mm wide for 50 Ω on the JLCPCB stack-up, set from Altium’s impedance profile and enforced by a dedicated width rule so it cannot drift during routing.

Designed around a 100 mAh cell

Wi-Fi peaks near 300 mA, so battery life depends on sleeping most of the time. The ESP32-S3 sleeps at a few microamps, and the two RT9080 regulators draw only about 2 µA idle, so the regulators do not undo the sleep current. USB pogo pads replace a connector for charging and programming to save thickness.

Machine assembly, staged bring-up

The QFN, LGA and 0201 parts are assembled by JLCPCB, not by hand. The bring-up plan is power rails first, then USB, then an I2C scan of the sensors, then Wi-Fi signal strength compared against a real XIAO.
